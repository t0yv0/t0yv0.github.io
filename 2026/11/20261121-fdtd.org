* FDTD: Maxwell's equations on a metal donut
<2026-11-21 Sat>

Let us use Maxwell's equations to visualize electrodynamic wave propagation. One of the
simplest computational methods to understand is a finite difference time-domain (FDTD) solver.

The solver approaches the problem very directly. Electric and magnetic fields at every point
in space are retained in computer memory. At each time step the solver computes a numerical
estimate of the curl of the H-field (magnetic) to update the E-field (electrical). The H-field
is updated similarly. What makes this a finite difference method is simply the use of small
discrete time steps (Δ t) and estimating derivatives by differences in values.

Instead of starting in full 3D generality we look at a 2D simplified problem first where there
is a "sheet" with X and Y axes for the magnetic field, and the electric field is assumed to be
perpendicular to this sheet along the Z axis pointing out of the page. The technical term for
this setup is transverse-magnetic (TMz) mode.

To assist with numerical stability, we use a Yee grid, a neat 1966 trick that samples E and H
fields at an offset of 1/2 cell width of each other. Time dimension is also offset to
"leapfrog" at Δt/2.

The derivatives simplify to:

$$ \partial_t E_z = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\left(\partial_x H_y - \partial_y H_x\right) $$
$$ \partial_t H_x = -\frac{1}{\mu_0}\,\partial_y E_z $$
$$ \partial_t H_y = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\,\partial_x E_z $$

To make things happen we initiate a sinusoidal wave source, and see how it interacts with a
metal donut that is modeled as a perfect electric conductor (~Ez = 0~).

Timesteps are chosen fine enough to satisfy 2D Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) stability
condition.

Apple MLX framework takes care of maximizing the performance we get on the M2 GPU cores by
dropping the computation into a Metal kernel. One eyebrow-raising hack here is calling
~mx.eval~ occasionally to flush delayed evaluation.

The whole timestep is a single ~@mx.compile~-d function. ~mx.roll~ builds each spatial difference
by shifting the grid, and ~mx.where~ pins ~Ez = 0~ on metal cells:

#+BEGIN_SRC python
def _update_h(hx, hy, ez, ch, dl):
    dez_dy = (mx.roll(ez, -1, axis=1) - ez) / dl
    dez_dx = (mx.roll(ez, -1, axis=0) - ez) / dl
    return hx - ch * dez_dy, hy + ch * dez_dx


def _update_e(hx, hy, ez, metal, ce, dl):
    dhy_dx = (hy - mx.roll(hy, 1, axis=0)) / dl
    dhx_dy = (hx - mx.roll(hx, 1, axis=1)) / dl
    return mx.where(metal, 0.0, ez + ce * (dhy_dx - dhx_dy))


@mx.compile
def step(hx, hy, ez, metal, src_mask, damp, ce, ch, t):
    hx, hy = _update_h(hx, hy, ez, ch, dl)
    ez = _update_e(hx, hy, ez, metal, ce, dl)
    ez = ez + mx.where(src_mask, amp * ramp * mx.sin(omega * t), 0.0)
    return hx, hy, ez * damp
#+END_SRC
